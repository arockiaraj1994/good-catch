## What happened

Many colleges now run students' essays through software that claims to spot writing done by AI. Students who wrote every word themselves are being flagged.

On 13 November 2026, six law students at Nepal Law Campus, part of Tribhuvan University, asked Nepal's Supreme Court to step in. They say the university relies on these checkers "without adequate research or a sound evidential basis". The lead student, Vivek Chaudhary, says one checker, iThenticate, marked 4 percent of an article he wrote himself as AI-written. He says when he ran real Supreme Court judgments through such tools, 70 to 80 percent was flagged.

On 21 November in Hyderabad, India's Chief Justice, Surya Kant, gave a speech on legal education. He said a 2023 study found that seven widely used AI checkers wrongly flagged more than three in five essays by non-native English speakers as written by a machine.

## Why it went wrong

These checkers guess from patterns in the writing. Simple, plain English, which is common when English is not your first language, can look "machine-like" to them. The tools give a number but can't explain it. Even Tribhuvan University's checker says its "likely AI-generated" result should not be taken as final.

## Impact

- Students risk failing work or being accused of cheating for writing they did themselves. One college in the report rejects assignments if more than 20 percent is flagged.
- Nepal's Supreme Court registered the case on 24 November and listed it for a first hearing. No decision yet.
- The Chief Justice said: "No student should therefore be held guilty of misconduct on the word of a machine alone."

## The lesson

1. **A score is not proof.** A machine's guess should never be the only reason to punish a student.
2. **Unfair to some more than others.** Students writing in their second language are flagged far more often.
3. **Talk to the student.** Asking them to explain and defend their work is a fairer test than a number no one can explain.

## What happened

In August 2024, someone tried to cash a suspected fake cheque at a bank in Florence, Kentucky, in the United States. A police detective, Ryan Kilmer, ran face-matching software, and it pointed to a man named Alain Vizcay Osorio.

Osorio was arrested in Florida in January 2025 and held for more than two weeks. Prosecutors later dropped the charges after he showed he was not at the bank when the cheque was handed over. He was working in Nebraska at the time.

Osorio sued the detective for false arrest. In November 2026 a federal judge, Chad Meredith, refused to throw the case out, so it can go ahead.

## Why it went wrong

Face-matching software only suggests people who look alike. The police department's own rules say a match is just a lead to follow up, not proof of who someone is. The lawsuit says the detective did not properly check that Osorio was really the man at the bank.

The judge wrote that the law does not allow an arrest "based on nothing more than an uncorroborated conclusion by AI" that someone committed a crime. He also said there was "good reason" to think no arrest warrant would have been given if the detective had explained how he reached his conclusion.

## Impact

- An innocent man spent more than two weeks locked up and faced charges for months.
- At this early stage, the judge said the detective is not automatically protected from being sued. The detective denies doing anything wrong, and no one has been found liable yet.
- This is not the first case like this. A lawsuit over a separate face-match arrest is under way in Fargo, North Dakota.

## The lesson

1. **A computer saying "this looks like him" is not evidence.** Someone has to check the facts before a person loses their freedom.
2. **Follow your own rules.** The police already had a rule that a face match is only a lead.
3. **Courts are starting to hold people to it.** Relying on the tool alone can now mean being sued.
